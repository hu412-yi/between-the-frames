Origins and Owners

Nine centuries of retreat, ownership and repair.

The classical gardens of Suzhou were rarely built by people at the height of their careers. They were built by people leaving them. Officials who had been dismissed, who had retired, or who wished to be seen as having chosen retirement turned a plot of land inside the city walls into an argument about how a life should be lived. The garden was a private world, and its walls, gates and windows were the instruments that kept it private while letting carefully chosen parts of it be seen.

The oldest surviving example sets the pattern. In 1044 the Song-dynasty poet Su Shunqin, removed from office, built a pavilion on the site of a former imperial flower garden and named it Canglang, after a classical verse about an honest man who withdraws from public affairs. The pavilion changed hands many times and fell into disuse after his death, was restored by the governor Song Luo in 1696, and was opened to the public in 1955. Today it is known, among other things, for its windows: more than a hundred of them, each with a different design.

The garden was an argument about how a life should be lived.

Canglang’s name comes from a verse in which an honest official withdraws from politics. Su Shunqin chose it after his own dismissal.

A succession of owners

Most Suzhou gardens are not the work of one person but of a chain of owners, each of whom bought, renamed, rebuilt and sometimes neglected what came before. The Master of the Nets Garden began in the Southern Song dynasty as the Ten Thousand Volume Hall of the official Shi Zhengzhi. After his death it passed through several hands and decayed. Around 1785 Song Zongyuan, a retired Qing official, restored and redesigned it and gave it the name it has now, which casts the owner as a fisherman: the conventional image of a man who has left office for a simpler life. In 1795 the scholar Qu Yuancun remodelled buildings and planted trees, and the garden received its first critical praise. It was finally given to the Suzhou government in 1958 by He Zehui, whose father had restored it in 1940.

The Humble Administrator’s Garden followed a similar course on a larger scale. Wang Xianchen, an imperial censor who retired from official life, began it in 1513 on the ruins of the Dahong Temple and completed it in 1526. After his death the property was divided. One part was lost by his son, others were bought and renamed through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and in 1860 the Taiping prince Li Xiucheng made it his residence and remodelled it. The layout visitors see today is said to derive largely from that period. The three parts were reunited only in 1949 and restored in 1952.

Painting as a first record

Some of what we know about how these gardens were meant to be seen comes from painting. In 1533 Wen Zhengming, a friend of Wang Xianchen and one of the leading painters of Suzhou, made an album of thirty-one views of the Humble Administrator’s Garden, each paired with a poem. Eighteen years later, at the age of eighty-one, he painted a second album of eight leaves, now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art. Its titles name single places rather than the whole: the Bamboo Bank, the Banana Balustrade, the Fishing Stone, the Locust Tree Pavilion.

Each leaf is itself a framed view. Wen does not survey the garden; he selects from it, the way a window in a wall selects from what lies behind. A fence, the edge of a roof or a bank of planting closes the scene, and a figure is placed so that we look where he looks. Paintings of this kind are not measured drawings and cannot tell us what a particular window looked like in 1551. They tell us something more general and perhaps more important: that the people who made these gardens already thought of them as a sequence of composed pictures.

Writing the rules down

In 1631 the garden designer Ji Cheng published Yuanye, usually translated as The Craft of Gardens. It is the first systematic treatise on Chinese garden making. Its third volume deals with doorways, windows and walls, together with paving, artificial mountains and the selection of rocks, and it ends with a chapter on jie jing, borrowed scenery. The order matters. By the late Ming dynasty, openings in walls were no longer treated as incidental details. They belonged to the same body of knowledge as the shaping of land and water, and they led directly into a theory of how a garden takes in the world beyond it.

Each leaf is itself a framed view.

Collectors, war and repair

The Lingering Garden shows how far a garden can travel from its beginnings. It was commissioned in 1593 by Xu Taishi, an official who had been impeached and later cleared, and built by the stonemason Zhou Shicheng as the East Garden. In the 1790s Liu Shu rebuilt it, completing the work in 1798, and filled it with distinguished rocks; an album of 1802 by Wang Xuehao recorded twelve of them. The Taiping war damaged it badly. The provincial treasurer Sheng Kang bought it in 1873, repaired it, and in 1876 renamed it Liu Yuan, the Lingering Garden. In the twentieth century it was abandoned twice, the second time serving as a horse-breeding ground during the war with Japan, before the city government took it over and reopened it in 1954.

Recognition arrived from outside as well as within China. Between 1980 and 1981 a courtyard modelled on one in the Master of the Nets Garden was built inside the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, using materials brought from China: nan wood columns, tiles from a kiln near Suzhou, Taihu rocks, and white walls pierced by windows with ceramic lattices, each of a different design. In 1997 UNESCO inscribed four Suzhou gardens on the World Heritage List, among them the Humble Administrator’s Garden, the Lingering Garden and the Master of the Nets Garden; in 2000 the listing was extended to five more, including Canglang and the Couple’s Retreat.

What a founding date does not tell us

This history has a consequence for anyone studying windows. A garden’s founding date is a beginning, not a date stamp for every object on the site. Walls were replastered, lattices were repaired or replaced, shell panes gave way to glass, and whole halls were rebuilt after war or neglect. The window in a photograph is the latest state of a long negotiation between owners, craftsmen and time. Reading it well means asking what it frames today, and also what it has framed before.

Before there is a window there is a wall. In a Suzhou garden it is usually whitewashed plaster over brick, topped with a coping of grey roof tiles that sometimes rises and falls like a wave. Against that pale surface everything else becomes visible: the dark outline of a doorway, the shadow of bamboo, the grey lattice of a window. The wall is not a background in the sense of something left over. It is the ground on which the garden’s compositions are drawn, and most of its work is done by staying closed.

An opening in such a wall is an act of editing. It decides what part of the garden returns to the eye and what stays withheld. Garden writers distinguish several kinds. A dongmen is a doorway without a door leaf, cut through the wall so that the path passes through it. An empty window, kongchuang, frames a scene without obstructing it. A leak window, louchuang, fills the opening with openwork of tile, brick or plaster, so that the scene behind is seen only in pieces. Canglang Pavilion has more than a hundred windows, and each has a different design.

Most of the wall’s work is done by staying closed.

Dongmen, doorway · kongchuang, empty window · louchuang, leak window.

A vocabulary of outlines

The outlines are drawn from a shared vocabulary. The circle of the moon gate is the most famous: a continuous curve with no corner to stop the eye, so the view is carried around its edge and gathered towards the centre. The octagon turns the eye at each of its eight corners. Others borrow the outlines of objects and flowers. A vase-shaped doorway plays on the word ping, which means both vase and peace, so that walking through it carries a small wish. Begonia and plum-blossom openings divide a circle into soft lobes, and read as flowers before they read as frames.

These shapes are not interchangeable. A doorway must be tall enough for a person, so its outline is stretched vertically and its lower edge often meets the ground. A window sits at eye level and is complete on all sides, so it can be a true circle or octagon. The same outline also changes meaning with its size. A small circle beside a corridor is a glimpse; a circle large enough to walk through is a threshold.

The thickness of a line

A drawing of a window is a line; a built window is a passage. The plaster wall has a thickness, and the opening is lined with a surround of finished brick or stone that the eye must cross before it reaches the garden. In a thin wall the scene seems to lie flat on the surface, almost like a picture hung on it. In a deep reveal the scene steps back. The surround catches light along its edge while its inner faces stay shaded, and this band of shadow makes the bright garden behind seem further away. The prototype above lets you change only that one dimension and watch the effect.

Depth also changes what you can see as you move. Standing straight in front of an opening you see the most. Step to one side and a deep reveal begins to cut the view, until only a sliver of the garden remains; a thin wall lets the scene slide past almost unobstructed. The wall therefore does not only frame a view. It decides from where that view can be had.

Gates for the body, windows for the eye

The difference between a doorway and a window is the difference between an invitation and a picture. A moon gate offers the scene beyond and also the route to it; the visitor can test the promise by walking through. A window offers the scene alone. It may show a rock you will reach only after several turns of a corridor, or a courtyard you will never enter at all. Placed in sequence along a covered walk, windows give a series of partial views that make the garden feel larger than its walls. Designers spoke of holding a scene back before revealing it, so that the eventual open view is felt more strongly.

This is why the photographs in this story keep the wall in the frame. Cropped to the opening alone, a window becomes a pattern that could belong anywhere, easy to collect and compare. Seen with its plaster, its coping and the path beside it, it is still a decision made in a particular place about what to show and what to keep closed.

A pattern is also an assembly. Behind the image of a flower window are pieces of material and the decisions that bring them together. A line on paper can pass through a corner without effort; a member of timber or tile must turn, end or join another piece. Looking at a Suzhou window as construction means asking, for every line in it, what it is made of and how it is held.

Three families of material are involved. The wall and the surround of the opening are masonry: brick laid in lime mortar, finished with plaster, the edges of the opening lined with carefully dressed brick or stone. Leak windows set into those walls are also masonry, their patterns built up from roof tiles, brick strips and plaster. Lattice windows and screens in the walls of halls and pavilions are timber, framed and jointed by carpenters. Within the timber, the small openings were filled with a translucent material: paper, thin panes of shell, and later glass.

For every line: what is it made of, and how is it held?

Tile, brick and plaster

Leak windows are made by masons, not carpenters. Their openwork is assembled inside the opening from pieces of curved roof tile, thin brick and mortar, then finished with plaster so that the whole pattern reads as a single carved surface. Curved tiles set back to back produce fish-scale and coin-like patterns; straight strips produce grids and frets. Because the parts are small and standardised, an enormous variety of designs can be built from the same few elements, which is one reason a single garden can contain dozens of different windows. When the Metropolitan Museum built its Suzhou courtyard in 1980–81, the white walls were pierced by windows with lattices of low-fired ceramic tile, no two alike.

Timber and joinery

The windows and screens of halls are timber. Suzhou’s garden authority stresses wood with good toughness, straight grain and few large knots for intricate window work, since a curved member cut from poor timber leaves a weak section exactly where the pattern is most delicate. The lattice is not drawn and then cut out; it is assembled from many short members whose joints are part of the design. Where two lines appear to cross, one member has been notched into the other; where a line seems to bend, two pieces meet. Seen up close, every pattern records a sequence of decisions about where a piece of wood can stop.

Shell, paper and glass

Before glass was common, the small openings of a lattice were covered with paper or with panes of shell. The windowpane oyster, Placuna placenta, has a flat, thin and translucent shell that has been used in place of glass for a very long time; in the Philippines it is still known as capiz. In Jiangnan windows the shells were ground into thin sheets and cut into small squares. Thin bamboo strips were fixed behind the timber lattice with small iron nails, and the shell squares were fitted between them. The size of the shell therefore helped set the spacing of the lattice: the geometry is partly a response to the material it holds.

Shell admits light while softening detail. It also ages. Panes can lose their translucency as they oxidise and may need to be repaired or replaced, so maintenance becomes part of a window’s construction history. Many windows that once held shell now hold glass, and the change alters both the light inside and the view out.

The makers

The craftsmen behind this work are usually anonymous, but their tradition has a name. Builders from Xiangshan, on the shore of Lake Tai near Suzhou, worked as carpenters, masons, plasterers, painters and rock-setters, and their tradition is still recognised today. In the fifteenth century the Xiangshan carpenter Kuai Xiang rose to direct imperial building works in Beijing. The anonymity of the garden window, in other words, hides a highly organised craft.

A pattern book without a book

Window designs travelled less through printed manuals than through practice. A master taught an apprentice how to set out a lattice, how to divide a frame and how to join its members, and designs were repeated, varied and combined from job to job. Ji Cheng’s Craft of Gardens of 1631 illustrates balustrade patterns and the outlines of doorways and windows, but most of the knowledge lived in workshops. That is why names such as ice-ray, coin, fish-scale or step-by-step brocade describe families of patterns rather than fixed designs: each craftsman adjusted the type to the opening, the material and the client in front of him.

A garden window is an instrument for light as much as for sight. It decides how much daylight enters a hall, from which direction, and in what form: as a bright field, as a soft glow through shell or paper, or as a pattern of light and dark thrown across the floor. Much of what makes these windows memorable happens not in the opening itself but on the surfaces around it.

From inside a hall the effect is often one of silhouette. The interior is darker than the garden, so the lattice reads as a black drawing laid over a bright ground, and every member is seen in outline. The photograph above is a clear case: the round frame and its star-like lattice become almost pure line. From outside, in daylight, the same window reverses. The lattice is lit and the interior behind it is dark, so the pattern appears pale against shadow.

The pattern appears twice: once in the window, once on the floor.

The second pattern

When sunlight passes through a lattice, the pattern appears a second time on the floor, on the opposite wall or on a table. This second pattern is never quite the same as the first. It stretches as the sun gets lower, shortens as it climbs, and slides across the room through the day. At the same hour in winter it reaches far into the hall; in summer, with the sun high, it stays close to the wall. A lattice designed as a flat geometry becomes, in use, a clock and a calendar.

The white garden walls work in the same way outside. Bamboo planted in a narrow court in front of a wall is there partly for its own sake and partly for the shadow it throws, which moves with the wind and changes with the hour. The wall becomes a screen, and the planting a drawing that is renewed every day.

Translucency

Shell and paper change the character of light rather than simply admitting it. Through them the garden is not seen; it is sensed as brightness and movement. A pane of shell glows when the sun falls on it and greys when a cloud passes. Glass, which replaced these materials in many windows, does something different: it lets the view through sharply and lets the light fall in hard-edged shapes. The change of material is therefore also a change of atmosphere, and one of the quieter ways in which the gardens have altered over time.

Photographing light

Light is the hardest thing in these gardens to record. A photograph fixes one hour, one exposure and one position, and it must choose whether to show the bright garden or the dark lattice, since the eye can hold both and the camera often cannot. The pictures in this story are partial in that sense. They show one state of a window that in reality is never still. The prototype above is a way of putting time back: it lets the same lattice be seen at many hours in a few seconds.

Eaves, orientation and the seasons

How much sun reaches a window depends on more than the window. Halls in Chinese architecture are commonly oriented towards the south, and their deep, overhanging eaves act as a seasonal filter. In summer, when the sun stands high at midday, the eaves shade the openings and keep the interior cool; in winter, when the sun is low, its light passes beneath the eaves and reaches far into the room. The same lattice therefore produces a short, compact pattern on a summer floor and a long, stretched one in winter. The architecture around the window decides when its pattern appears at all.

Water and planting add a further layer. Light reflected from a pond can play across a ceiling or the underside of eaves, moving with every ripple, while a tree in front of a window breaks direct sun into a scatter of small bright spots before it ever reaches the lattice. In the gardens, the shadow of a window is rarely a clean geometric figure. It is mixed with the shadows of leaves, the reflections of water and the soft glow of translucent panes, and it changes from minute to minute.

Reading the prototype

The model above simplifies all of this into a single wall, a single lattice and a single sun, so that one relationship can be seen clearly: how the angle of the light turns a flat pattern into a moving figure. Choose a dense lattice such as the ice-ray and the floor fills with small, irregular cells; choose the open window and a single bright shape travels across the room. Set the season to winter and the pattern reaches towards the viewer; set it to summer and it retreats to the foot of the wall. None of these images is the window itself. Each is one of the many ways the window exists through the day.

A view begins from somewhere. Move a little and the near edge, the distant tree and the roof no longer meet in quite the same way. The window remains where it is, while its contents seem to shift. Suzhou’s gardens are built on this simple fact. They are not designed to be seen from one point but walked through, and their windows and gates are placed so that the walk becomes a sequence of changing pictures.

Ji Cheng ended The Craft of Gardens in 1631 with a chapter on jie jing, borrowed scenery: bringing into the garden things that lie outside it. The classic example is visible from the Humble Administrator’s Garden, where the Pavilion Leaning Against the Rainbow frames the Beisi Pagoda, a Buddhist tower some distance beyond the garden wall. The pagoda belongs to the city, not the garden, yet from the right place it becomes the culmination of a garden view. The frame does not create the pagoda. It creates the relationship.

Jie jing, borrowed scenery, is the subject of the final chapter of Ji Cheng’s Yuanye (1631).

A different view with each step

A common phrase describes the experience: yi bu yi jing, a different view with each step. The Lingering Garden has some seven hundred metres of covered corridors, many with windows along one side. Walking them, the visitor sees the garden through a rhythm of openings: a rock, then a fragment of water, then a tree, each composed for a moment and then lost. No single photograph records this, because the experience is the change itself.

Windows also change what is easiest to notice. A branch seen through a lattice disappears behind a member and reappears in the next interval, so its direction becomes visible in a way it would not be against open sky. A small amount of visible material suggests a form larger than the frame can contain, and the mind supplies the rest. The partial view is not a lesser version of the full one; it is a different way of seeing.

A garden that keeps changing

The scene inside a frame changes on several timescales at once. Within minutes, the light moves and the wind stirs the planting. Within a year, the trees leaf, flower and drop, and the same window shows bare branches, blossom and dense green. Over decades, trees grow into views or are lost, halls are rebuilt, and new owners rearrange what their predecessors set up. Today another change is obvious: the gardens are full of visitors, and the people standing in front of a hall or crossing a moon gate have become part of the view.

Returning to the same window therefore offers another encounter rather than a recovery of an identical picture. The frame is the constant; what it holds is always provisional. That is perhaps the deepest lesson of these openings. They do not fix the garden into an image. They give a changing world a stable edge against which its changes can be seen.

Holding back, then opening up

Garden designers often withheld a view before granting it. A visitor might enter through a narrow, dim passage, pass a closed wall with only a leak window or two, and turn a corner before the main pond and its halls opened out. The Lingering Garden is known for exactly this kind of approach: a long, turning entrance corridor that keeps the garden hidden until the visitor reaches a window and then an open view across the water. The contrast makes the open scene feel larger and brighter than it would if seen at once. Windows are the hinges of such sequences. They promise without delivering, and in doing so they shape the moment of arrival.

The same logic operates on a small scale. A single leak window set in a long white wall turns a plain walk into a sequence of glimpse, interruption and glimpse again. Each opening is a pause, and each pause is composed. What the visitor remembers is rarely one picture but the rhythm of closed and open, dark and bright, near and far.

From view to archive

This website ends where the book ends, with the visitor making a frame of their own. The prototype above fixes your chosen outline and lattice in a wall and lets you move the garden behind it until the composition feels right. Saving it turns that moment into a plate with an archive number, the same way a photograph turns a walk into a single image. The plate is not the garden. It is a record of one relationship between a frame and a view, chosen by you, which is exactly what the windows of Suzhou have always offered.

How the final animation works

The book’s cover slides a landscape behind a fixed window. This animation does the same thing at the scale of a building: the windows stay still and you move.

It is a single continuous camera move along a covered corridor, thirty-four metres long, with a whitewashed wall on one side. The wall is pierced by five windows, one for each story and in the same order: the octagon of Origins and Owners, the circle of Wall and View, the begonia of Materials and Making, the square of Light and Shadow and the vase of A Changing View. Each carries the lattice that story discusses, and each frames a view from the gardens in this archive.

Scrolling sets the time. Play walks at a slow pace, about forty seconds from end to end, with the camera turning towards each window as it passes and away again, so that every view is composed for a moment and then lost. A low afternoon sun stands behind the wall, and the lattices are drawn a second time across the corridor floor. At the last window, the vase, the walk stops and the frame becomes the title of the book.

For the presentation, Record video saves the same walk as a video file that can be placed in a slide.
